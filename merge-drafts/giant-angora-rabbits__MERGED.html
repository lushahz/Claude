The Giant Angora is the largest of the four ARBA-recognized Angora breeds — there is no maximum weight, senior does must weigh at least 10 pounds, and many adults exceed 12 pounds under a coat that makes them look even bigger. It is also the highest-yielding of the ARBA Angoras: a well-maintained Giant from a productive line typically produces 28 to 40 ounces (790 to 1,130 g) of wool a year, with exceptional individuals producing more.

It is a non-molting breed. Unlike the English, French, and Satin Angoras, the Giant Angora’s coat does not shed on its own and will grow without limit unless harvested, so shearing every 90 days is a non-negotiable welfare requirement, not a recommendation.

This guide covers everything about the breed in one place: its American origin, the ARBA standard, how big Giant Angoras get compared with the other Angora breeds, the three-component coat, wool yield, grooming, diet, housing, health, and whether the breed suits you.

History and Origin of the Giant Angora

The Giant Angora is not an ancient breed. It is a distinctly American creation, developed in the second half of the 20th century by a single breeder with a specific purpose.

Louise Walsh of Taunton, Massachusetts, began her breeding program with the goal of creating a large Angora rabbit that would qualify for ARBA recognition as a wool-producing breed. The obstacle she faced was that ARBA had previously declined to recognize the German Angora — at the time the highest-producing Angora wool breed — on the grounds that its body type was too similar to the English Angora. This is documented in the Angora rabbit entry on Wikipedia, which draws from the ARBA Standard of Perfection.

Walsh’s response was to engineer a fundamentally different body type. She crossed German Angoras with French Lops and Flemish Giants — both large commercial-type breeds — to add substantial frame, bone structure, and body weight to the German Angora’s non-molting, high-yield wool genetics. After nearly three decades of selective breeding to fix the desired traits — large commercial body, three-component non-molting coat, gentle temperament, and consistent wool yield — she successfully petitioned ARBA for recognition. The ARBA Giant Angora breed page confirms the breed was developed specifically as a wool producer with a three-component coat that must be shorn, as the breed does not molt. ARBA officially recognized the Giant Angora in 1988, making it the first new Angora breed accepted by ARBA in many decades.

ARBA Breed Standard: Key Specifications

The Giant Angora is judged on wool density, texture, uniformity, and quantity alongside body size and conformation. The non-molting coat means show animals are assessed on a maintained coat rather than the molted coat state seen in English and French Angora show presentation.

Physical Characteristics

Body

The Giant Angora has a commercial body type on the largest scale of any Angora breed — heavily muscled, broad across the hindquarters, with a full loin and deep body. The large body mass is what enables the breed’s high fiber yield. A Giant Angora at full adult weight and in full coat is a substantial animal with significant physical presence.

Wool Coverage

The Giant Angora’s coat covers the body fully with a multi-layered structure. The face is not fully wooled — it has head furnishings and cheek trimmings, but not the complete facial coverage of the English Angora. The ears carry wool trimmings. The overall impression is of a very densely wooled body with a face that shows clear rabbit features.

Fiber Specifications

The Giant Angora’s typical annual yield of 28 to 40 ounces is the highest of the four ARBA-recognized Angora breeds — roughly two to three times what a productive English or French Angora (12 to 16 oz each) produces. (The IAGARB-governed German Angora, which is not ARBA-recognized, generally produces more.)

The non-molting coat characteristic means the fiber does not self-release and must be cut. Fiber that is not harvested on the 90-day schedule becomes increasingly dense, prone to tip matting, and eventually forms solid mats that require cutting in sections. Allowing a Giant Angora coat to exceed the 90-day harvest window is a welfare issue, not a scheduling inconvenience.

How Big Do Giant Angora Rabbits Get?

Under the ARBA standard there is no upper weight limit. Senior bucks must weigh at least 9.5 pounds (4.3 kg) and senior does at least 10 pounds (4.5 kg); well-conditioned Giants commonly exceed 12 pounds (5.4 kg). That makes the Giant the largest Angora, although the other breeds vary widely too:

The Coat-Size Illusion

Every Angora in full coat looks far bigger than it is. Angora fiber grows about 1 inch (2.5 cm) a month, so by the end of a 90-day cycle the coat stands 3 inches or more off the body on every side, and the full-coat silhouette can be 8 to 10 inches wider than the rabbit underneath. A freshly shorn Giant looks surprisingly lean and angular, and many first-time owners are startled by how much of the “size” was wool. Plan housing around actual body length and weight, not full-coat appearance.

How Long It Takes a Giant Angora to Reach Full Size

The molting Angora breeds (English, French, and Satin) reach adult body size at around six to nine months. Giants grow much more slowly: does typically take more than a year to mature and bucks up to 18 months. A nine-month-old Giant that is still well below the adult standard is not necessarily unhealthy. Compare it with breed-appropriate growth for its age, and ask a rabbit-experienced vet if you are unsure.

The Three-Component Coat: What Makes It Unique

The Giant Angora’s coat structure is shared only with the German Angora among all Angora breeds. It consists of three distinct fiber types that must be present in the correct balance for the coat to receive full points at an ARBA show.

Underwool is the dominant component. It is medium-fine, soft, delicately waved, and carries a gentle shine. It forms the bulk of the harvested fiber and is the primary driver of wool weight at each shearing. The ARBA standard and the National Angora Rabbit Breeders Club (NARBC) both specify that underwool must be dominant over the other two fiber types.

Awn Fluff sits above the underwool. It is a stronger, wavy wool with a characteristic hooked or curved tip — a guard hair tip on a wool-type fiber. It is found between the underwool and the awn hair layers and contributes body and structure to the coat.

Awn Hair is the outermost component — straight, strong guard hairs that protrude above the surface of the coat. The awn hair must be present and evident in a correct Giant Angora coat. Too little awn hair produces a coat that lacks structure and mats more readily. Too much produces a coarser, stiffer coat that scores poorly on texture.

This three-component architecture is what distinguishes the Giant Angora coat from the predominantly underwool coats of the English, French, and Satin Angoras. The result is a coat that is somewhat easier to manage than the all-underwool English Angora coat — the awn hair helps keep the coat from collapsing and felting — but still requires strict maintenance on a 90-day shearing schedule.

In judging Giant Angoras at ARBA shows, the majority of points are awarded for wool — covering density, texture, and length. General type — body conformation, head, ears, eyes, feet, and legs — accounts for the remaining points. This weighting reflects the breed’s primary purpose as a wool producer.

The 90-Day Shearing Requirement: Why It Is Non-Negotiable

The Giant Angora’s wool grows continuously without self-limiting. Unlike molting breeds where the old coat releases naturally and creates the harvest window, the Giant Angora’s coat simply grows longer, denser, and heavier with every passing week beyond the 90-day mark.

Beyond 90 days without shearing:

  • Coat weight increases and restricts comfortable movement
  • Tip matting begins as the outer fiber layer becomes too long to maintain structural integrity
  • The coat begins to hold moisture, urine, and debris more effectively — increasing flystrike risk
  • Progressive mat formation accelerates until solid mats require cutting in sections and cannot be fully combed out

The NARBC Standards of Care and IAGARB Standards of Care both specify mandatory 90-day shearing for non-molting Angora breeds. This is not an optional recommendation — it is the welfare standard for this breed.

Newly shorn Giants lose a great deal of insulation overnight, so watch them closely for chilling in the first week or two after each clip, especially in cooler months. Many breeders keep small rabbit coats on hand for that period.

Grooming

Giant Angora grooming has two components: the ongoing maintenance grooming between shearing sessions, and the shearing itself every 90 days.

Ongoing Maintenance Grooming

Minimum: Two to three full grooming sessions per week between shearing appointments.

Grooming order: Always begin at the underside — belly, chest, inguinal folds — before moving to back, sides, legs, and head furnishings.

Giant Angoras require particular attention to the hindquarters and inguinal fold area between shearing sessions. The density of the coat in these areas makes them high-mat-risk sites where solid mats can develop within days of a missed session.

Shearing

Giant Angora shearing every 90 days requires either competence with small animal clippers or access to an experienced Angora rabbit groomer. Key safety considerations:

  • Rabbit skin is thin and tears easily — keep the clipper blade flat against the skin at all times
  • Move slowly in areas with skin folds — inguinal folds, dewlap, armpits
  • Use blunt-nosed scissors around the face and any area with limited visibility
  • Two people — one to hold the rabbit steady, one to shear — significantly reduces injury risk

The harvested fiber from each shearing should be skirted immediately — removing any second cuts (short fiber pieces created when scissors pass over an area twice), soiled material, and debris — before storage.

Tools Required

  • Small animal clippers — mandatory for 90-day shearing
  • Steel grooming comb — between-session maintenance
  • Slicker brush — surface finishing
  • Blunt-nosed scissors — detail work around face and sensitive areas
  • Pet grooming blower on cool setting — highly recommended for coat management between shearing sessions

Diet

The Giant Angora’s large body mass and high fiber yield create the highest nutritional demand of any Angora breed.

Unlimited timothy hay — non-negotiable. The single most important dietary element for all rabbits.

Minimum 17% protein pellets — confirm on the label. Provide approximately ¼ cup per 5 lbs of body weight daily, adjusted for individual condition. At full Giant Angora adult weight of 10 to 12 pounds, this represents approximately ½ to ¾ cup of pellets per day — more than smaller Angora breeds.

Daily fresh leafy greens — romaine, cilantro, parsley, dandelion greens.

Fresh water at all times — a rabbit producing this volume of fiber has elevated hydration requirements. Monitor water consumption and ensure the sipper bottle or crock is functioning and accessible.

Housing

The Giant Angora’s body size requires larger housing than other Angora breeds.

Heat stress is a particularly acute concern for the Giant Angora. The non-molting, continuously growing dense coat provides insulation that cannot be shed — unlike molting breeds that naturally reduce coat volume in warm months. Maintaining indoor temperature below 75°F (24°C) during warm months is the conservative management standard for this breed.

Health

GI Stasis

The primary emergency for all rabbit breeds. Any Giant Angora not eating or not producing droppings for 6 to 8 hours requires same-day veterinary assessment. The large body size means GI stasis progresses with potentially greater severity in Giant Angoras than in smaller breeds — the volume of intestinal gas that can accumulate is proportional to body size.

Heat Stress

The Giant Angora’s non-molting, continuously dense coat is a significant heat stress risk factor that does not reduce between shearing sessions the way molting breed coats do during summer. This breed should never be housed in unventilated spaces, near heat sources, or in rooms that exceed 75°F (24°C) during warm months.

Wool Block

The high volume of fiber this breed carries and self-grooms, means wool block risk is present year-round. Unlimited hay is the primary preventive measure. Consistent grooming reduces the volume of loose fiber available for ingestion.

Sore Hocks

The Giant Angora’s body weight places greater pressure on the hind feet than smaller breeds. Wire flooring without adequate solid resting board coverage accelerates sore hock development. A solid resting board appropriately sized for the Giant Angora’s larger foot surface is essential and must be present at all times.

Giant Angora vs. Other Large Angora Breeds

Prospective owners frequently compare the Giant Angora with the German Angora — the other large, non-molting, three-component coat Angora breed. The key differences are practical and worth understanding clearly.

The German Angora produces more wool annually than the Giant Angora. The Giant Angora holds ARBA show eligibility and has a significantly larger body mass. For owners whose primary goal is ARBA showing alongside serious fiber production, the Giant Angora is the clear choice. For owners focused purely on maximum wool output without ARBA show requirements, the German Angora warrants consideration.

Temperament and Suitability

The Giant Angora is a calm, docile breed that tolerates handling well when handled from a young age — a necessity, given that it will be handled extensively for grooming and shearing throughout its life. It is not suited to owners who cannot commit to the 90-day shearing schedule and the two-to-three-times-per-week between-session grooming.

Well suited to: Experienced rabbit owners with a clear fiber production goal; owners who want maximum annual fiber yield from each rabbit; homesteaders building a small commercial Angora fiber operation.

Not suited to: First-time rabbit owners; owners who travel frequently without reliable, experienced care coverage; owners without access to small animal clippers or a skilled groomer for 90-day shearing.

FAQs

How big does a Giant Angora rabbit get?

There is no upper weight limit for the Giant Angora under ARBA standards. Senior bucks must weigh a minimum of 9.5 pounds (4.3 kg), and senior does a minimum of 10 pounds (4.5 kg). Many individuals from established lines weigh 12 pounds or more. The dense wool coat makes the animal appear even larger than its actual body weight.

When is a Giant Angora rabbit fully grown?

Later than other Angoras. Most Angora breeds reach adult body size by six to nine months, but Giant Angora does usually take more than 12 months and bucks up to 18 months.

How much does an Angora rabbit weigh?

Adult Angora rabbit weight ranges from 5 to 7.5 pounds for the English Angora, 7.5 to 10.5 pounds for the French Angora, 6.5 to 9.5 pounds for the Satin Angora, and 9.5 pounds or more (no maximum) for the Giant Angora. German Angoras range from 5.5 to 12 pounds. All figures are for adult animals at full body size.

How often does a Giant Angora rabbit need to be sheared?

Every 90 days without exception. This is a non-negotiable welfare requirement — not a recommendation. The non-molting coat grows without limit and causes progressively serious welfare problems if not harvested on schedule.

How much fiber does a Giant Angora produce per year?

Typically 28 to 40 ounces (790 to 1,130 g) a year across four 90-day shearings from a well-maintained rabbit from a productive line, with some exceptional individuals producing more. That is the highest annual yield of the four ARBA-recognized Angora breeds.

What colors are recognized for the Giant Angora?

Ruby-eyed white (REW) has long been the standard variety. A Chestnut variety was reported accepted at the 100th ARBA Convention in 2023, so check the current ARBA Standard of Perfection for the latest color list before buying show stock.

How does the Giant Angora compare to the German Angora?

Both breeds are large, non-molting, and must be clipped every 90 days. The key differences are ARBA recognition — the Giant Angora is ARBA-recognized and showable, the German Angora is not — and wool yield, where the German Angora produces more wool per year. Body type also differs: the Giant Angora has a large commercial-type body, while the German Angora has a more compact loaf shape.

Can Giant Angora rabbits live outdoors?

No. The non-molting, continuously dense coat creates elevated heat stress risk that indoor climate control mitigates. Additionally, the coat felts in moisture, flystrike risk is substantially higher outdoors, and consistent 90-day shearing scheduling is incompatible with outdoor housing conditions.

What size enclosure does a Giant Angora need?

At least 36 × 30 inches of floor space, and larger is better. The enclosure must let the rabbit take three full hops in any direction and stand fully upright without touching the ceiling.

Is the Giant Angora a good pet?

Giant Angoras have notably gentle temperaments and can be excellent companions. Their care demands are significant, but owners who meet those demands consistently typically find them highly rewarding. They are not suitable as a first rabbit for inexperienced owners.

Is the Giant Angora good for first-time owners?

Not recommended. The 90-day mandatory shearing requirement, the scale of the ongoing grooming commitment, and the body size management demands make the Giant Angora most appropriate for experienced rabbit owners with a clear fiber production objective.

How long do Giant Angora rabbits live?

Typically 7 to 10 years. Like most large rabbit breeds, Giants tend toward the lower end of the Angora lifespan range, and missed 90-day shearings are one of the most common ways that life gets cut short. See our Angora rabbit lifespan guide.

Where can I find a Giant Angora breeder in the United States?

The ARBA maintains a breeder directory at arba.net. The National Angora Rabbit Breeders Club at nationalangorarabbitbreeders.com is also a resource specifically for Angora breed connections. ARBA-sanctioned shows are an excellent venue for meeting active Giant Angora breeders in person. For general guidance, see our Where to Buy an Angora Rabbit guide.

Conclusion

The Giant Angora is the ultimate fiber production rabbit within the ARBA system — producing more fiber per animal per year than any other recognized breed, in a calm and handleable package that tolerates the extensive grooming and shearing its coat demands. The mandatory 90-day shearing schedule is its most significant management requirement and the clearest dividing line between owners prepared for this breed and those who are not.
